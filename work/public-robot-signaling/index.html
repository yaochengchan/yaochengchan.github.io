Why robot signals that work on video get missed in the hallway

A three-phase research program on how pedestrians make sense of autonomous robots in shared spaces, and how to design signals people actually notice.

My role
Lead researcher, from study design through analysis
Team
Living and Working with Robots group, UT Austin
Scale
n = 411 across three phases
Methods
Online video experiments, field deployment, interviews, grounded theory
Platforms
Clearpath Husky, Boston Dynamics Spot
Tools
SPSS, Python, Qualtrics

The problem

Robots now share public space with people who never asked to meet one. Teams need to know which signals actually communicate what the robot intends to do.

What I did

Compared five signaling designs online, field-tested the strongest on a live robot in a busy hallway, and interviewed pedestrians afterward.

The result

Gaze cues that won on video were missed entirely in the field. What worked were bright signals in the forward view and familiar cues like a handler or a vest.

The problem

When autonomous robots work in pedestrian spaces like sidewalks, hallways and lobbies, they share the space with bystanders who have no training and no reason to expect a machine. If the robot can't make its intentions clear, people hesitate, collide with it, or simply feel uncomfortable.

Teams designing these robots kept asking the same thing: which signals actually work? Should the robot use eye gaze, lights, arrows or body language? And do the answers we get in controlled tests still hold on a busy sidewalk?

The questions I set out to answer

  1. Intent: Which signals (gaze, light patterns, arrows) best tell pedestrians where a robot is going?
  2. Lab vs. real world: Do the benefits seen in controlled video studies hold when people meet a live robot in a busy, distracting hallway?
  3. Context: How do visible signs of human oversight, such as a handler, a joystick or a service vest, change how safe and comfortable people feel?

My role

I was the lead researcher and owned the work end to end: framing the questions, designing all three phases, running the online studies and field deployment, analyzing the data, and writing up guidelines. I worked with roboticists in UT Austin's Living and Working with Robots group, who handled robot autonomy and operation.

Approach

I sequenced the work so each phase cut risk for the next. Cheap online studies narrowed the options, the field test checked them in reality, and interviews explained the why.

  • Phase 1

    Controlled online video studies

    Compared five signaling designs (puppeteered head, Nao gestures, arrow pointers, LED strips, animated eyes) using standardized measures, including the Perceived Social Intelligence (PSI) scale.

  • Phase 2

    Field deployment in public

    Ran a Clearpath Husky through an active university hallway with scheduled participants, measuring how close people got, how they steered around it, and their immediate reactions.

  • Phase 3

    Interviews & thematic analysis

    Post-encounter semi-structured interviews, analyzed with grounded theory and hybrid thematic coding, to understand how bystanders make sense of autonomous machines.

What we tested

1 · Signals for direction (Clearpath Husky)

Ways to show which way the robot is heading and that it has noticed an oncoming pedestrian.

2 · Expressive movement (Boston Dynamics Spot)

Can dog-like movements (a tail wag, a play bow, sitting to wait) make a quadruped robot less intimidating than standard mechanical walking?

3 · Signs of human oversight

Does seeing that a person is responsible for the robot change how comfortable people feel?

What we found

Attention is the bottleneck. On video, subtle cues like robotic eye gaze clearly raised perceived social intelligence and softened negative reactions when the robot made mistakes. In a busy hallway, pedestrians were too occupied with their own route to notice the gaze at all.

  • Subtle cues fail in distracting places. Shown the footage afterward, participants could read the robot's gaze, but in the moment they missed it. Signals need to sit in people's forward line of sight and be bright or physically obvious.
  • Expressive movement makes robots seem warmer. The dog-like movements significantly increased how lifelike and approachable people found the robot (Godspeed questionnaire), offsetting the intimidating look of a bare mechanical quadruped.
  • Familiar cues lower perceived risk. Cues people already understand, like a handler with a leash or a service vest, instantly answered the question every bystander has: "What is this robot doing here, and who's responsible for it?"

What this means for product teams

01

Prioritize obvious signals in the forward view over subtle ones

Eye gaze and small head tilts work in focused, one-on-one interactions. A robot moving through shared space needs high-contrast signals people can catch at a glance, like light strips or floor projections.

02

Build on roles people already understand

Bystanders hesitate because they can't tell what the robot is for. Clear signs of its role and of human oversight, such as badging, status lights or a visible operator, build trust right away.

03

Validate outside the lab

Lab and unmoderated video tests overrate subtle cues because participants are looking only at the screen. Testing in the real setting catches edge cases and shows how little attention people actually have.
