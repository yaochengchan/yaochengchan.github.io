When can a cheap video study stand in for an expensive field test?

A research framework that lets teams use fast, cheap video studies to rule out weak designs and plan the field test that follows.

My role
Co-author: methodology and experimental validation
Team
UT Austin (Hauser, Modak, Biswas, Hart)
Scale
Online N = 128+, plus field deployment
Methods
Between- and within-subjects design, power analysis, mixed methods
Tools
G*Power, SPSS, R, Qualtrics
Output
Framework published at RO-MAN 2024, plus an open dataset

The problem

Field tests with real robots are slow and costly. Video studies are fast, but nobody knew when you could trust them.

What I did

Co-developed a framework that links video studies to a specific field setting, then used variability from the online data to size the field study.

The result

Video matched the direction of real-world reactions but overestimated how much attention people pay, so teams now know what video can and can't tell them.

The problem

Every team testing physical products faces the same trade-off: speed versus realism. In-person tests of robots, hardware interfaces or spatial computing are slow, complex and expensive. Online video studies and digital prototypes are fast and scale easily, but you can't be sure the results will hold in the real world.

Without a principled way to connect the two, teams either over-invest in field tests or ship decisions based on lab results that don't hold up.

My role

I co-developed the methodology with colleagues at UT Austin and led the experimental validation: study design, power analysis, and analysis of the online and field data.

The framework

Vid2Real links video studies to a specific real-world setting, so the cheap test is designed from the start to predict the expensive one.

Stage 1

Scope the field

Define the real location, how people move through it, the lighting and the edge cases before filming anything.

Stage 2

Matched video study

Film from camera angles that match the site, then test many design variants with a large online sample.

Stage 3

Calibrate

Estimate effect sizes from the online data, calculate the field sample size, and drop weak variants.

Stage 4

Targeted field test

Deploy only the strongest conditions and check what carries over to the real world.

Validating it

We tested the framework with a two-part study of how pedestrians react to a mobile robot using its baseline walking behavior versus expressive body language and spoken requests.

  • Online video study (N = 128+): a controlled between-subjects experiment measuring perceived social intelligence, how lifelike the robot seemed, and whether people intended to comply with its requests. Expressive movement showed significant effects, which gave us the numbers to size a live study with 95% power.
  • Field deployment: ran the strongest conditions with a real robot in a busy campus corridor. The direction of people's reactions matched the video results, and the field test revealed attention effects that video had missed.

Key insight: Video studies are strong for comparing how people perceive designs and for sizing samples. But they consistently overestimate attention, because in the real world people are busy.

Guidance for research teams

01

Use video to rule out weak options

Don't take a large set of untested variants into an expensive field trial. Use matched video studies to narrow down to the two or three strongest.

02

Size the field study from the online data

Field studies in shared spaces usually need a different group of participants for each condition. Use the variability measured online to calculate the minimum sample size you need.

03

Match the camera to the real point of view

How you film shapes the results. A first-person view predicts personal discomfort and how close people are willing to get, while an onlooker's view inflates how sociable the robot seems.
