Mapping the journey of a robot that has to ask strangers for help

A Boston Dynamics Spot waited at a busy campus entrance and asked passersby to open the door. I mapped what happened from two sides: the pedestrian who never chose to meet a robot, and the operator running its hardware and control software.

My role
Lead researcher: study design, field observation, analysis, journey synthesis
Team
Living and Working with Robots group, UT Austin
Setting
Main entrance of a busy campus building, live one-hour session
Methods
Field observation, Wizard-of-Oz teleoperation, informal conversations with passersby
Platform
Boston Dynamics Spot, teleoperated
Output
Two-lane journey map, design opportunities, ACM/IEEE HRI 2025 paper

The problem

Robots in public spaces sometimes need help from people who have no reason to give it. Teams usually design for the operator and forget the bystander.

What I did

Ran a live field observation of a robot asking strangers to open a door, then mapped the experience stage by stage for both the pedestrian and the operator.

The result

Most failed requests weren't refusals. They were misses: people never noticed, or didn't think the robot meant them. Every stage pointed to a fix.

Why map two journeys

A robot that delivers food or patrols a building depends on people it was never designed for. The operator chose to use it. The person walking past did not. If the robot needs a door opened or a path cleared, its success rests on that second person.

Product teams usually map the operator or customer journey and treat everyone else as background. I wanted to see the encounter through both sets of eyes at once, so a fix on one side wouldn't create a problem on the other.

The setup

The building entrance had two doors side by side: a manual door and an automatic door with an accessibility button. Both led through a double-door vestibule. Spot couldn't get in alone.

Operator

Watches from inside

Two operators teleoperate Spot through the glass. An observer takes notes outside.

Robot

Waits at the entrance

Spot stands just outside the door, out of the main walking path.

Encounter

Turns and asks

As someone approaches, Spot walks toward the door, faces them and says, "Can you please open the door for me?"

Outcome

Enters or resets

If helped, Spot goes inside. If not, it tries someone else or walks back to its start position.

What I found

The robot lost people at "notice," not at "decide." Spot's verbal and body cues kept visible negative reactions low. Most people who didn't help weren't unwilling. They were busy, or didn't think the request was meant for them.

  • Attention is scarce in shared space. Headphones and group conversations filtered out a voice request entirely. Some people had to take off their headphones to understand what the robot wanted.
  • People pick the cheapest way to help. Helpers almost always used the accessibility button. It let them start the door and walk away without holding two doors for a robot.
  • Context shapes willingness. Many passersby had robotics backgrounds and read the scene as an experiment to leave alone. How a robot's role is signaled changes whether people engage.
  • The operator's workaround is a product requirement. Turning off obstacle detection got Spot through the door but moved safety onto human vigilance. A deployed robot can't rely on that.

What this means for product teams

01

Map the bystander, not just the operator

Any connected device in shared space, from delivery robots to cameras and intercoms, has an operator who chose it and people nearby who didn't. Both journeys decide whether it works.

02

Design for divided attention

Assume people are distracted. Use cues that work in their forward view, without sound, and that make clear who is being addressed.

03

Make the right action the easy action

When people choose how to help, they choose the lowest-effort option. Design the ask around that path instead of fighting it.
