How much personality should a service robot (and its screen) have?

Designing and testing coordinated gesture and tablet-UI personalities for a customer-service robot, and finding that the right amount depends on what's at stake.

My role
Lead interaction designer and experimental researcher
Team
National Chengchi University, Taipei (Dr. Shih-Yi Chien, Dr. Yi-Ling Lin)
Scale
N = 255, factorial lab experiments
Methods
Multimodal prototyping, controlled lab experiments
Platform
SoftBank Pepper, Choregraphe, Python, tablet UI
Measures
Willingness to use, trust, purchase intent, profile recognition

The problem

Customer-facing robots and kiosks need a communication style people trust, and the industry was stuck choosing between just two: extrovert or introvert.

What I did

Designed three coordinated profiles (extrovert, ambivert, introvert) across gestures, gaze and tablet UI, then tested them in low- and high-stakes service tasks.

The result

Gestures plus screen beat screen-only, ambivert rated highest overall, and higher financial risk made users much pickier about personality.

The problem

When a robot or kiosk handles customer service, such as hotel check-in, retail help or financial advice, its communication style shapes whether people trust it, enjoy using it and come back.

Earlier systems offered only two styles, extrovert or introvert. I introduced a third, ambivert, and asked how a robot's physical behavior and its on-screen interface should work together, especially as tasks get more complex and the financial stakes rise.

My role

I was the lead interaction designer and experimental researcher. I designed the three behavior profiles across body movement and tablet UI, built them on the Pepper robot, and designed and analyzed the controlled lab experiments.

The design system

To make each personality recognizable, I coordinated movement, gaze and screen layout into three consistent profiles:

ExtrovertAmbivertIntrovert
GesturesLarge, sweeping arm motions; fast; wide head nodsBalanced gestures suited to the moment; moderate speed and nodsSmall motions close to the body; slow; minimal nodding
Gaze & distanceConstant eye contact; open posture; stands closerGaze moves naturally between screen and user; neutral, upright postureOften looks down; leans in cautiously; keeps more distance
Tablet UILarge type, bold headers, high-contrast highlightsClear sections, consistent type hierarchy, balanced spacingBulleted lists, small type, little visual emphasis

What we found

Risk amplifies personality. In routine, low-stakes tasks, users barely cared about personality. As complexity and financial risk rose, they strongly preferred the extrovert and ambivert robots, and trust and purchase intent dropped sharply for the introvert.

  • Gestures plus screen beat screen alone: combining body movement with tablet content significantly beat text-only screens on willingness to use, likeability and perceived social intelligence.
  • 83% recognition: in blind trials, participants correctly identified the extrovert and introvert profiles 83% of the time, confirming the designs came across as intended.
  • The ambivert advantage: the ambivert profile scored highest overall on likeability and sustained willingness to use, balancing approachability with professionalism.

What this means for product teams

01

Match assertiveness to the stakes

For high-stakes decisions like payments, medical intake or complex onboarding, a quiet, reserved interface makes people more anxious. Use confident, clear cues such as steady gaze and structured summaries to show competence.

02

Keep body and screen in sync

Gestures should point to on-screen content. When movement and screen content share the same pace, the interaction takes noticeably less mental effort.

03

Default to ambivert in mixed-use services

Extroverted behavior grabs attention, but nonstop high energy wears people out over longer sessions. An ambivert default stays clear without being overwhelming.
