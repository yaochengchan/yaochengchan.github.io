Why robot signals that work on video get missed in the hallway

Delivery and service robots now share hallways and sidewalks with people who never expected to meet one. I led a three-phase program to find out which signals pedestrians actually notice and understand.

OutcomeEye-gaze cues that raised ratings on video went unnoticed in a busy hallway. The program produced signaling guidelines for robots in public spaces (n = 411).

A low-cost robot head that let us test behavior before autonomy existed

Building autonomous gaze software first would have stalled user testing for months. I co-built a puppeteering rig: an operator moves a handheld controller and the robot head mirrors it in real time.

OutcomeStudy setup went from hours of software calibration to minutes. Testing showed that two axes of motion (pan and tilt) are enough to signal where a robot is paying attention.

When can a cheap video study stand in for an expensive field test?

Field studies with real robots are slow and costly, and online video studies are fast but can mislead. I co-developed a framework that tells teams when video is good enough and how to size the field study that follows.

OutcomePublished framework and open dataset. Video studies matched the direction of real-world reactions but overestimated how much attention people pay.

How much personality should a service robot (and its screen) have?

I designed three communication profiles that coordinate a humanoid robot's gestures with its tablet interface, then tested how each one shapes trust and willingness to use it in customer-service tasks.

OutcomeGestures plus tablet UI beat screen-only on every measure. The balanced 'ambivert' profile rated highest, and the higher the financial stakes, the stronger users' preferences became (N = 255).

How I work

From an open question to a decision

Most of my projects start with a question nobody can answer yet. Here's how I get it to something a team can build on.

Frame the decision

Start from what the team needs to decide, not from a favorite method. Then rewrite the open question as one I can test, with clear criteria for success.

Pick the cheapest method that's still valid

Use video studies, Wizard-of-Oz rigs and low-fidelity prototypes to rule out weak options early, before spending on hardware or field time.

Test in the real context

Lab results can mislead. I check findings where the product will live (hallways, sidewalks, service counters), where people have less attention to spare.

Turn findings into requirements

Hand off design guidance that engineering and design can act on, with the evidence and confidence level behind each item.

For product teams

  • Scoped an MVP to unblock the team. We built a simple puppeteering rig instead of waiting for autonomy software, so user testing could start right away.
  • Built a way to prioritize designs: rule out weak ones cheaply online, then spend field time only on the strongest. See Vid2Real →
